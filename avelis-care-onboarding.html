Lesson One

The Avelis Standard.

What it means to work here — and why it matters.
"Most home care agencies fill shifts. Avelis builds relationships. That distinction is not marketing — it is the operating philosophy that shapes every decision we make, including this one: choosing you."

You have been selected to join Avelis because we believe you bring something that cannot be trained in a classroom — genuine patience, attentiveness, and the kind of human warmth that people living with dementia can sense immediately, even when words fail.

But even the best caregivers need a framework. The Avelis Standard is ours. It is not a set of rules — it is a way of working that separates what we do from what most agencies provide.

The Avelis Standard
Consistency over convenience. Presence over procedure. Showing up — reliably, calmly, without fail — every single time.

Here is what that looks like in practice:

i.
You Are Not Interchangeable
We match you to clients deliberately — because the relationship between caregiver and client is the care. You are not a body filling a shift. You are a trusted presence.
ii.
Consistency Is Therapeutic
For someone living with dementia, a familiar face is not a nicety — it is a clinical necessity. You showing up the same way, every time, is itself a form of care.
iii.
Dignity Is Non-Negotiable
Every person in your care is someone's entire world. They deserve to be treated as full human beings — with history, preferences, and inherent worth — on their worst days as much as their best.
iv.
You Represent Something Larger
Every interaction you have reflects on Avelis and on the families who trusted us. Excellence is not occasional — it is the baseline. We hold ourselves to it because families deserve nothing less.

Other agencies have caregivers. Avelis has standards. You are here because we believe you are capable of upholding them — and because we believe you want to.

Lesson Two

Dementia-Informed Care.

Understanding the condition so you can meet the person.
"Dementia is not one experience. It shifts, it surprises, and it asks more of a caregiver than any diagnosis summary can prepare you for. What it requires most is a willingness to enter someone else's world — without correction, without urgency, without distress."

Before you can care for someone living with dementia, you need to understand what they are experiencing. Not clinically — but humanly. Their reality may not match yours. That is not confusion to be corrected. It is a world to be honored.

Here is what Avelis caregivers understand that sets them apart:

  • Memory loss is not the whole picture. Emotional memory often remains intact long after factual memory fades. A person may not remember your name but will remember how you made them feel. Show up with warmth every time — it registers, even when nothing else does.
  • Their reality is valid. If someone believes it is 1962 and they need to pick their children up from school, do not argue. Enter their reality gently. Redirect without dismissing. The goal is calm — not accuracy.
  • Behavior is communication. Agitation, repetition, and resistance are almost always expressions of an unmet need — fear, pain, confusion, or discomfort. Your first question should always be: what is this person trying to tell me?
  • Routine is medicine. Familiar sequences reduce anxiety. The same greeting, the same sequence, the same calm presence creates a sense of safety that nothing else can replicate. This is why consistency is our highest value.
  • Every day is different. Good days and difficult days follow no predictable pattern. Come prepared for both. Anchor yourself in patience rather than expectation.
A Note on Dignity
"Never talk about a person in care as if they are not in the room. Never speak to them as you would a child. They are adults with full lives behind them — honor that, always."

You will encounter moments that are hard. Repetitive questions, moments of fear or anger, resistance to personal care. These are not failures — they are the nature of the condition. Your composure in those moments is the care. A regulated caregiver regulates the person they're with. That is not a metaphor. It is neuroscience.

Knowledge Check
A client you've been caring for insists that her late husband is coming home for dinner and begins to get distressed when you suggest otherwise. The best response is to:
a.Gently but firmly explain that her husband passed away, as honesty is always the best policy
b.Acknowledge her feelings, enter her reality, and gently redirect toward a comforting activity
c.Change the subject immediately to distract her from the thought
d.Contact the family immediately to report the episode
Correct. Meeting someone in their reality — acknowledging feelings first, then gently redirecting — is the dementia-informed approach. Correcting creates distress. Connection creates calm.
In dementia care, correcting someone's reality almost always increases distress without benefit. The priority is always emotional comfort, not factual accuracy. Try again.

Lesson Three

Communication & Presence.

The art of being genuinely there.
"Presence is not proximity. Being in the same room is not the same as being truly there. Avelis caregivers are trained to be attentive — not just available."

How you communicate with the people in your care is as important as anything else you do. In dementia care especially, tone, pace, and body language carry more weight than words. Here is what excellent communication looks like in practice:

i.
Slow Down
Speak slowly, calmly, and in short sentences. Allow time for processing — more than feels natural. Rushing communicates anxiety, and anxiety is contagious.
ii.
Get at Eye Level
Approach from the front, not behind. Sit when possible. Eye contact that is warm and steady communicates safety in ways words cannot.
iii.
Use Names
Use their preferred name — ask the family what that is. Names orient. They communicate: I know who you are. I see you. You matter.
iv.
Ask One Thing at a Time
Multiple questions or choices create overwhelm. "Would you like to sit in the garden?" is better than "Would you like to go outside, or maybe watch TV, or we could take a walk?"
On Touch
"A gentle hand on the arm can communicate more safety than an entire conversation. Use touch deliberately, warmly, and always with awareness of the individual's comfort."

Companion care — one of our core services — is built entirely on the quality of your presence. It is not about filling silence. It is about unhurried attention — following the person's energy, being genuinely curious about their stories, and allowing conversation to go wherever it goes.

Ask about their life. People living with dementia often retain vivid long-term memories. A question about where they grew up, what they did for work, or who they loved can open something extraordinary. You are not just passing time. You are honoring a life.

Knowledge Check
You arrive for a shift and your client seems withdrawn and won't make eye contact. The best approach is to:
a.Give them space and check in every 30 minutes until they seem more receptive
b.Sit nearby, speak softly, and try a topic or activity you know they enjoy — without pressure
c.Ask directly what's wrong so you can address the problem
d.Proceed with the care plan regardless of their mood
Well done. Calm, warm, low-pressure presence — meeting them where they are without forcing engagement — is the right approach. Patience is the tool.
Withdrawal in dementia often responds best to gentle, non-pressuring presence. Giving too much space, asking direct questions about feelings, or proceeding without awareness can all increase distress. Try again.

Lesson Four

Navigating Difficult Moments.

Composure is the care. Here is how to hold it.
"The moments that are hardest are the ones that matter most. Your ability to remain grounded — calm, unhurried, and genuinely present — when a client is distressed is the highest expression of this work."

Difficult moments are not exceptions in dementia care — they are part of the territory. Sundowning, agitation, repetitive questions, resistance to personal care, confusion about identity or time. These will happen. How you respond is the difference between escalation and ease.

Here are the most common challenging situations and how Avelis caregivers approach them:

Scenario
Repetitive Questions
"A client asks you what time her daughter is coming — for the fourteenth time in an hour."
Avelis approach: Answer warmly every time, as if it is the first. The question is almost always expressing an underlying need — usually anxiety or a desire for reassurance. Respond to the feeling behind it: "She'll be here soon — and in the meantime, it's just you and me. You're in good hands." Then gently redirect to something engaging.
Scenario
Resistance to Personal Care
"A client refuses to shower and becomes verbally agitated when you approach the bathroom."
Avelis approach: Do not force it. Safety first — yours and theirs. Step back, give space, and try again in 20–30 minutes when the agitation has passed. In the meantime, consider whether the approach itself can change — a different time of day, a preferred song playing, a more gradual introduction. Note the episode and discuss with the family. Never attempt personal care against clear resistance.
Scenario
Sundowning
"At 4pm, your client becomes increasingly confused, anxious, and wants to leave the house to 'go home.'"
Avelis approach: Do not argue or block physically. Walk with them if possible — movement helps. Acknowledge the feeling: "I can hear you want to go home. Let's take a little walk together." Redirect gently toward a familiar, comforting activity afterward. Keep lighting warm and environment calm. Note patterns and share with the family — sundowning at the same time each day is important clinical information.
Always Remember
"If you ever feel unsafe, or if a situation escalates beyond what you can manage alone — contact Avelis immediately. You are never expected to handle a crisis without support."
Knowledge Check
A client becomes physically agitated and refuses personal care. The correct immediate response is to:
a.Proceed quickly to minimize the length of distress
b.Step back, give space, ensure safety, and try again later with a different approach
c.Call the family immediately and wait for instructions before doing anything
d.Document the refusal and skip personal care for the day
Correct. Safety first, always. Step back, allow the agitation to pass, then try again with a thoughtful, different approach. Force is never the answer.
Proceeding against clear resistance is never appropriate, and skipping care entirely without an attempt is not the Avelis standard either. The right approach is to step back, ensure safety, and try again. Try again.

Lesson Five

Working With Families.

The people behind the care — and how to serve them too.
"When you walk through a client's door, you are not only caring for one person. You are giving an entire family permission to breathe. That is a profound responsibility — and a profound privilege."

Family caregivers are often exhausted, grieving, and carrying more than they show. The person who hired Avelis may be a spouse who hasn't slept well in months, or an adult child managing this from another city while holding down a career and raising their own children. Your presence in their home is the relief they've been waiting for.

How Avelis caregivers work with families:

  • Honor their knowledge. The family knows this person better than anyone. Their instructions and preferences are not suggestions — they are essential information. Listen carefully. Ask thoughtful questions. Never dismiss what they tell you about their loved one.
  • Communicate clearly and promptly. After each visit, give a brief, honest update. What went well. What was difficult. Anything worth noting. Families should never have to wonder how the visit went. This is how trust is built — and maintained.
  • Stay in your lane. You are there to provide non-medical, companionship and respite care. You are not a nurse, a therapist, or a medical advisor. Never offer diagnoses, medical opinions, or advice that falls outside your role. If you observe something concerning, report it to Avelis and let us determine the appropriate response.
  • Be a calm presence for the family too. Some family members will be emotional — occasionally directed at you. Do not take it personally. Lead with empathy. Understand that what you're witnessing is grief and love, not criticism of you.
  • Maintain professional boundaries. Deep relationships with families are natural in this work. But Avelis caregivers do not accept gifts of significant value, do not borrow from clients or families, and do not engage in financial discussions with the people in their care.
If Something Concerns You
"Never try to manage a serious concern about a client's wellbeing or safety alone. Contact Avelis directly — that is exactly what we are here for. You will never be penalized for raising a concern."
Knowledge Check
After a shift, a family member asks you whether you think their mother's dementia is getting worse and whether she should see a specialist. You should:
a.Share your honest observations and suggest they speak with her physician
b.Reassure them that everything seems fine to avoid causing worry
c.Acknowledge the question, share your observational notes, and encourage them to contact Avelis and the client's physician for guidance
d.Decline to comment as it is outside your role entirely
Exactly right. You can share what you've observed — that's valuable — but you direct medical questions to the appropriate professionals. Avelis is your partner in navigating these conversations.
Offering a medical opinion (even well-intentioned) or dismissing the concern are both problematic. Share your observations, stay within your role, and connect the family with the right support. Try again.

Your Role & Responsibilities.

What we expect — and what you can expect from us.
"This is a two-way commitment. Avelis will support you, communicate with you, and place you with intention. In return, we ask that you hold the standard — in every visit, with every family, without exception."

As a 1099 independent contractor, you set your own schedule within the assignments you accept. But every assignment you accept carries the full weight of the Avelis name and standard. Here is what that looks like day to day:

i.
Reliability Above All
If you accept a shift, you are there — on time, prepared, and present. Last-minute cancellations directly harm vulnerable people and fracture the trust families have placed in us. Contact Avelis immediately if an emergency arises.
ii.
Professional Appearance
Arrive clean, dressed appropriately, and without strong fragrances. Sensory sensitivity is common in dementia — strong smells can cause significant agitation. Your appearance communicates professionalism to families on their most stressed days.
iii.
Phone Use
Personal phone use during a shift is limited to urgent matters. Your full attention belongs to the person in your care. This is non-negotiable and reflects directly on Avelis in every home you enter.
iv.
Confidentiality
Everything you learn about a client and their family — their condition, their home, their dynamics — is strictly confidential. This includes social media. A single breach of privacy ends your relationship with Avelis immediately.

Your compensation: Training pay for completing this onboarding is included in your first paycheck following your first completed shift. Your hourly rate was discussed during your placement — if you have questions about compensation, contact Avelis directly.

  • Training pay is included in your first check after your first completed shift assignment
  • Communication — all updates, scheduling changes, and client notes go through Avelis directly
  • Support — you can reach Avelis any time a situation feels beyond what you can manage alone. We mean this.
  • Feedback — we will check in with you after your first several shifts. Your experience matters to how we operate
A Final Word
"We hired you because we believe in you. The families who will welcome you into their homes are trusting us with something irreplaceable. Carry that with you — and know that we are carrying it alongside you."
